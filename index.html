Your traps

From my wrong answers, 7 to 12 Oct

  1. Trap 1 · CIA wording · 7 Oct

    Disclosure is a loss of confidentiality, not integrity. Integrity = modification or destruction.

  2. Trap 2 · FIPS 199 names · 7 Oct

    Only Low, Moderate, High are levels. "Limited" and "Serious" are adjectives inside the definitions. Low = Limited · Moderate = serious · High = severe or catastrophic (the Horrible one). Missed again in the 13 Oct mock (chose Moderate for "severe").

  3. Trap 3 · Defence emphasis · 7 + 9 Oct

    Passive attacks → prevent. Detect and recover is for active attacks, so the integrity service is about detection. Passive changes nothing, so there is nothing to detect.

  4. Trap 4 · Publishers · missed 3× · right 2/2 on 11 Oct

    RFC → IETF, always. X.800 → ITU-T. FIPS 199 → NIST.

  5. Trap 5 · Threat vs attack · right 2/2 on 11 Oct

    "Derives from an intelligent threat" is the attack definition. A threat is only the potential.

  6. Trap 6 · SEE vs CHANGE · 9 Oct

    The attacker wants to see it (contents, or who talks to whom and how often) → confidentiality. They change it (modify, insert, reorder, replay) → integrity.

  7. Trap 7 · Replay · 9 Oct

    Replay is active. Ask which step causes the harm: the resending. It works even on encrypted messages, so it's integrity, not confidentiality.

  8. Trap 8 · "Source" · 9 Oct

    "The source of a single message, e.g. email" → data origin authentication, not access control.

  9. Trap 9 · Fail-safe vs complete mediation · 9 Oct

    Default answer is NO ("block unless allowed") → fail-safe defaults. Check every time → complete mediation.

  10. Trap 10 · Open design · 9 Oct

    Publish the algorithm, keep the key secret → open design.

  11. Trap 11 · Separation vs least privilege · 9 Oct

    Is there a "two" (two keys, password and code)? → separation of privilege. About how far one person's access reaches → least privilege.

  12. Trap 12 · Attack-surface categories · 10 Oct

    Only N-S-H: Network, Software, Human. Human IS a category. Anything else (e.g. "cryptographic") is the NOT answer.

  13. Trap 13 · Surface × depth · 11 Oct

    Count the bad things: large surface and shallow layering. 0 bad → low, 1 bad → medium, 2 bad → high. Large + deep is medium, not low.

  14. Trap 14 · Reading attack trees · 11 Oct

    Labels (P/I, difficulty) are given facts, don't argue with them. Read each node as a sentence: OR = "either… or", AND = "both… and". Defender: AND → block one child; OR → block all children.

  15. Trap 15 · Optimistic security · 10 Oct

    Tolerate temporary violations that are easy to detect and correct, when the violator is accountable. "Never allow any deviation" is the pessimistic opposite.

  16. Trap 16 · Origin vs nonrepudiation · 11 Oct · right 2/2 on 12 Oct

    "Came from" / "source" → data origin authentication (convinces the receiver). "Can't deny" / "prove it to a third party" → nonrepudiation.

  17. Trap 17 · Where is the data? · 12 Oct

    A message travelling between two parties → in transit (eavesdropper, middle-person). Sitting in storage (disc, backup tape, stolen drive) → at rest. A device busy computing (timing, power, sound, voltage glitch) → in use (side channel, fault injection). No message on a wire = not in transit.

  18. Trap 18 · Authenticity vs accountability · 12 Oct

    Account-ability = keep an account: actions traced to you, logs, forensics ("who did it?", after). Authentic-ity = genuine, verifiable, trusted source ("is it real?", at the door).

  19. Trap 19 · Message vs machine · missed 4× on 13 Oct

    Eavesdropper hears the message on the network. Side channel hears the machine: power, timing, sound, heat or EM are always side channel. Café example: recording the clicks of a keyboard to get the password = side channel. "Silently captures / reads" = eavesdropper; "relays / alters" = middle-person. Exam rule: timing, power, heat, sound/noise, fan, EM or CPU behaviour in the question → side channel, always, even with a microphone and even if they get the key. Judge by what they listened to, not what they got.

  20. Trap 20 · Port, IP, MAC · 13 Oct

    The first letter: Port = which Program (80 web, 22 SSH). IP = where on the Internet (start to end). MAC = the Machine next door (next hop only). A router reads the IP. Read layer names, never the numbers.

  21. Trap 21 · Which key? · 13 Oct

    Secret → lock with their public key (only their private opens it). Signature → sign with your private key (everyone checks it with your public). Signing with a public key proves nothing: everyone has it.

  22. Trap 22 · PGP order · missed 3× on 13 Oct

    ACCESS with the two Cs swapped: sign → compress → encrypt → radix-64 → segment. The E is E-mail compatibility (Encode, radix-64), not encryption: encryption lives inside C (Confidentiality). Any option that encrypts before compressing is wrong (compression removes the patterns, so it must come first).

  23. Trap 23 · Web of trust vs PKI · missed 3× on 13 Oct

    Web of trust = Web: bottom-up, any user signs any user's key, no CA. PKI = Pyramid: top-down CA hierarchy. "CA / root / hierarchy" in an option = PKI, never web of trust.

  24. Trap 24 · PGP segments · 13 Oct

    Signature and session key appear once, typically in the first segment. They are stuck on the front, and segmentation is the last step (scissors don't copy).

  25. Trap 25 · Two sequence numbers · 13 Oct

    TCP sequence number = order. IPsec AH sequence number = anti-replay (S = Stop resends).

Slides 30–32

Passive vs active attacks

"Once you start changing things, you start to be an active attacker."

Passive: learn, don't touch

  • Release of message contents: reading the email, call or file
  • Traffic analysis: contents encrypted, but who, how often and how long are still visible

Very hard to detect, because nothing changes.

Defence: PREVENT (encryption)

Active: alter or disrupt

  • Masquerade: pretend to be someone else
  • Replay: capture a valid message, resend it later
  • Modification: alter, delay or reorder messages
  • Denial of service: stop normal use

Too hard to prevent outright.

Defence: DETECT + RECOVER
Lecturer's DNS example

Listening to DNS queries = passive. Changing the returned IP to send the victim to a fake site = active.

Slides 24, 41–47 · X.800

The five security services (+ availability)

A service is the promise ("nobody can read this"). A mechanism is how the promise is kept (encryption).

ServiceThe promiseWatch for
AuthenticationThe party or message is who it claims to bePeer entity: both sides of a live connection (e.g. two TCP modules); stops masquerade and replay. Data origin: the source of one message, suits email, and does not stop duplication or modification
Access controlLimits who can reach which hosts and applicationsNeeds authentication first, so rights can be tailored to each user
Data confidentialityProtects data from passive attacks: nobody can see itTraffic-flow confidentiality hides source, destination, frequency and length
Data integrityReceived exactly as sent: nobody changed itConnection-oriented = the whole stream: no duplication, insertion, modification, reordering, replay or destruction (so it also helps against DoS). Connectionless = single messages, modification only. Active attacks → detect, not prevent
NonrepudiationNeither side can deny the messageBoth directions: proof of who sent it, and proof that it was received
Availability serviceKeeps the system usableCounters DoS. X.800 treats it as a property of the other services
Campus picture

The card reader checking that the card is really yours = authentication. The list of rooms your card opens = access control. A signed, dated form that proves you handed in your coursework = nonrepudiation.

Encryption, digital signatures and the rest are mechanisms, never services.

Surface × depth (slide 52)

Small surfaceLarge surface
Deep layeringLow riskMedium risk
Shallow layeringMedium riskHigh risk
Count the bad things

Large surface = 1 bad thing. Shallow layering = 1 bad thing. 0 → low, 1 → medium, 2 → high. Shrink the surface (fewer doors) and deepen the layering (more locks behind each door): the two complement each other.

Slides 53–59

Attack trees

A branching, hierarchical data structure that represents a set of potential techniques for exploiting security vulnerabilities.

Parts
  • Root: the attacker's goal (the security incident)
  • Branches: subgoals, refined step by step
  • Leaves: different ways to initiate the attack
  • Branches can be labelled with difficulty, cost or risk to compare paths
AND vs OR
  • OR: any one child is enough. Defender must block every child
  • AND: all children are needed. Defender blocks one child and the branch dies

Week 2 · L02 slides 14–20

Day in the life: DHCP → ARP → DNS → TCP → HTTP

You type www.google.com, but computers route by IP address. The Domain Name System turns the name into the number.

  1. "Who am I?" DHCP (Host Configuration) gives the laptop its own IP, the first-hop router's IP and the DNS server's IP. Broadcast to everyone, over UDP (quick)

  2. "Who's next door?" ARP (Address Resolution) turns the router's IP into its MAC. Before DNS, before HTTP

  3. "Where's Google?" DNS (Domain Name) turns the name into Google's IP. The query goes in UDP → IP → Ethernet

  4. "Can we talk?" TCP handshake: SYN → SYN-ACK → ACK

  5. "Give me the page." HTTP request and reply

The 3-way handshake

#MessageMeans
1 · laptop →SYN (synchronise)"Hi, can we connect?"
2 · ← GoogleSYN-ACK"Got it (ACK). Hi back (SYN), can you hear me?"
3 · laptop →ACK (acknowledge)"Yes, got yours."

Why three: after step 2 the laptop knows both directions work, but Google doesn't know the laptop got its reply. The ACK tells it (captions l.378–418). Only TCP (reliable) handshakes; UDP (quick) doesn't, which is why DHCP and DNS use it.

Passive risk

Plain DNS isn't encrypted, so an eavesdropper sees every site you look up.

Active risk

Spoofing or poisoning the reply sends you to a fake site.

SYN flood (background)

Thousands of SYNs, never the final ACK: the server's half-open connections fill up and real users can't connect. Active, DoS, hits availability.

Defences such as DNSSEC and DNS over HTTPS, and the SYN flood, are background knowledge, not on the slides.

Week 2 · L02 slides 23, 31, 46 + captions

Hashing vs encryption

A hash function turns any amount of data into a short, fixed-size value (a message digest). It's one-way: you can't get the data back. Same data → same hash; a tiny change → a completely different hash. So it gives integrity, not confidentiality.

Lecturer's analogies

Paint: yellow + blue = green, but from the green you can't tell how much of each went in. Counting the Es in a long text: the count lets you check the text, but it can't rebuild it.

Output sizeFixed (SHA-1 = 160 bits, SHA-256 = 256)About the input's size
Gives youIntegrity (detects change)Confidentiality

Trap: MAC ≠ MAC address

In AH and SSL, MAC = message authentication code. In the data-link header it's a hardware address.

Why both?

Encryption keeps the data secret; the hash shows whether it was changed (captions l.916–922).

"Hashing is a very big topic" (l.882), but NSS only wants the top layer: one-way, detects change, not the same as encryption.

Week 2 · L02 slides 26–41 + captions

PGP: Pretty Good Privacy

By Phil Zimmermann (open standard: OpenPGP), for email and data storage. A hybrid cryptosystem: a random symmetric session key encrypts the content, and the receiver's public key encrypts that session key. It protects the message at the application layer, "before it is stored, forwarded, or read" (slide 28).

Hook: ACCESS

The 5 services (slide 29): Authentication · Confidentiality · Compression · E-mail compatibility · Segmentation. The run order is ACCESS with the two Cs swapped.

StepServiceWhat happens
1 SignAuthentication160-bit SHA digest, encrypted with the sender's private key, prepended to the message
2 CompressCompressionZIP, "after appending the signature but before encryption"
3 EncryptConfidentialityOne-time 128-bit session key (CAST-128 default, IDEA, 3DES; CFB mode). Session key encrypted with the receiver's public key (RSA/ElGamal)
4 Radix-64E-mail compatibilityBinary → ASCII (radix-64 = Base64), because email is character-oriented
5 SegmentSegmentationSplit long messages (limit can be 50,000 octets); signature + session key appear once, in the first segment
Which key does what

Sign = Sender's private (checked with the sender's public). Session key = locked with the Receiver's public. Content = symmetric session key, so public keys are used only "indirectly for confidentiality".

Why compress before encrypt

Cryptanalysis hunts patterns ("good morning", lots of the letter E; that's how Enigma fell). Compression replaces repeats with symbols, removing them: an "extra layer, extra protection" (captions l.714–754).

Key ID = the least significant 64 bits of the public key (8 bytes). It says which key was used, because people can have several. ⚠ Two 64s: radix-64 (encoding) and the 64-bit key ID.

Web of trust (slides 40–41)

PKI = Pyramid

Top-down: a hierarchy of certificate authorities (CAs) signs keys. Like a passport: one authority vouches for you.

Web of trust = Web

Bottom-up: "a user's public key can be signed by any other user". If A fully trusts Zaza and Zaza signs Toto's key, A may trust Toto. Friends = direct trust, friends' friends = indirect.

Slide 28: the algorithm names are historical, so "focus on the workflow". Lecturer: PGP is "quite old but still very, very much in use".

Week 2 · L02 slides 42–57 + captions

IPsec: IP security

Security at the network layer, so it covers all applications: "we're not waiting for the application". Three areas: authentication, confidentiality, key management. Biggest use: VPNs. Built into IPv6, can be used with IPv4.

Why: traffic analysis (said twice)

Encryption hides what is said, not who talks to whom. A CEO's IP talking to a rival CEO's IP is sensitive on its own, "so we need to protect the IP headers". That's what tunnel mode does.

Security Association (SA)

A one-way logical connection: two-way traffic needs two SAs. Identified by 3 things: the SPI (32-bit, local significance only), the IP destination address and the security protocol identifier (AH or ESP).

IKE: Internet Key Exchange

Sets up the SA and keys before AH/ESP can be used. Combines ISAKMP (framework), Oakley (Diffie-Hellman key; cookie exchange stops the DH clogging attack) and SKEME (re-keying). IKE = UDP port 500.

"You can read" slides (49–52)

SAD vs SPD

SAD (SA Database) = the details of each SA: SPI, sequence counter, anti-replay window, AH/ESP info, lifetime, mode (tunnel / transport / wildcard), path MTU. SPD (Policy Database) = the rules: which traffic gets which SA, matched by selectors (IPs, protocol, name, ports).

SPD actions

Policy → Protect, Bypass, Discard. UDP 500 = IKE → bypass. TCP 443 to the server → bypass, because TLS already encrypts it (avoid double encryption).
